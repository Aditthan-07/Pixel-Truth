## Abstract
The ability of a person to distinguish a synthetic picture from a real one just by looking at it is now decent, and this can have major implications for misinformation, digital forensics, and general trust in pictures. In this paper, **PixelTruth**, a tiny web app, will be described that analyzes an uploaded image to determine if it is a true photograph or a machine-made image and presents a saliency heatmap to the user explaining this. Instead of training a new classifier, the system simply reuses two pre-trained classifiers: the first, umm-maybe/AI-image-detector, is a ViT model trained on early GAN and VQGAN+CLIP imagery, while the second, Organika/sdxl-detector, is a Swin Transformer fine-tuned specifically for Stable Diffusion XL output, and then combines their votes using a simple majority-plus-confidence-averaging rule. The saliency module is then activated using a gradient-based method to point out the pixels that most affected that classification. All of this is driven by a Flask REST API, and a browser-based front end, making for a full and reasonably repeatable client-server pipeline. While it also illustrates the design, implementation and the behaviour of a functional evaluation of the system, it does point out one of the interesting mismatches: the explainability feature is described in the project as "Grad-CAM", but the explanation it computes is actually an input-gradient saliency map, a closely related, but different approach, with different visual properties.

**Keywords** — *AI-generated image detection, Vision Transformer, Swin Transformer, ensemble learning, explainable AI, saliency map, Grad-CAM, image forensics, Flask, transfer learning.*

## I. Introduction

With the rise of GAN-based generators, diffusion models, and large-scale text-to-image systems such as Stable Diffusion, Midjourney, and DALL-E, photorealism is no longer a good indicator of authenticity. When a viewer scrolls past an image on the internet, there is no easy way to find out if the picture is taken by a camera, or generated by a neural network, and that's an issue that has much to do with misinformation, impersonation, fabricated evidence and a general loss of trust in what people see. There are automated solutions which can flag potentially synthetic images, and offer a reason, not just a verdict -- and this is a useful addition to the digital forensics and content-moderation toolbox.

One of the reasons it is difficult is because it's a very dynamic field. The desire to capture the "tricks" of one generator type and then attempt to control another type that the wielding generator has never encountered in training, is common in the literature reviewed in the following section and is often repeated here. Instead of gaming one coverage, it is what motivated the approach taken here, that is, treating the detection as a more like a systems problem; how to harness existing detectors, wrap them in something usable, and communicate a decision (and its rationale) to someone who is not a machine learning specialist? This paper is about creating a full-stack Web application around that concept: **PixelTruth**. It first categorizes an uploaded image into a real photo or an AI-generated image by two separate pretrained transformers, and then it overlays a gradient-based saliency map on the image and presents the classification result together with the saliency map to the user as a number and a map, instead of the result being simply a number. The system simply loads two pretrained checkpoints from Hugging Face and combines their predictions using an easily manually verifiable fusion function and makes them available via a REST API that receives a tiny little front end built in a browser.

The contributions here are:
1. A two-model ensemble, fusing an earlier GAN-era detector with a detector fine-tuned specifically for Stable Diffusion XL, using majority voting with confidence-score averaging as a tie-breaker.
2. An input-gradient saliency module to show the pixels contributing to the top-scoring model of the ensemble.
3. An honest appraisal of how the system performs in practice and an acknowledgement of the limitations of ensembling two detectors with different biases.
4. A deployable client-server architecture that ensures the entire exchange remains stateless, with no image data permanently written to disk.
5. A functional investigation of the system's performance, coupled with a candid discussion of the limitations of such an ensemble.

The structure of the remaining paper is organized as follows: In Section II, related research for detecting synthetically-generated imagery and explainability in general is explored. In Section III, the fusion methodology is presented in detail. The architecture of the system, its implementation, and the experimental setup are covered in Sections IV, V, and VI, respectively, with results and discussion presented in Section VII. The limitations are discussed candidly in Section VIII, Section IX covers the ethical aspects and responsible use of such a tool, Section X presents future work, and Section XI concludes the paper.

## II. Related Work

The research areas of interest for detecting AI-generated images are more broadly characterized as forensic artifact analysis, learned classifiers trained directly on the problem, and, more recently, detectors based on top-tier general purpose vision features.

Wang et al. showed that, given the right augmentation strategy -- one that felt almost too effective -- a classifier trained on only one GAN family could generalize surprisingly well to GAN families it had never seen during training -- and this made CNN-generated-image detection more of a classic transferable classification task than a search for architecture-specific forensics [1]. However, this optimism was short-lived as diffusion models surpassed GANs in image quality. To ascertain whether the ability to detect objects learned in the GAN era can be transferred to the diffusion output, Corvi et al. tested whether this is possible and concluded that it did not work out well for many generator families with substantial performance degradation that required retraining or fine-tuning on diffusion-generated images to restore reliable detection [2]. Ojha et al. went one step further with a frozen general-purpose vision-language feature instead of using classifier-specific artifacts, assuming that a more general-purpose representation would be more suitable across generators [3]. The same generalization gap is the rationale behind PixelTruth merging two detector networks, rather than using a single one -- umm-maybe/AI-image-detector has been trained on previous GAN and VQGAN+CLIP era imagery, while Organika/sdxl-detector has been fine-tuned specifically for SDXL output, with the idea that two is better than one to cover more of the generator space than any individual detector.

Both PixelTruth's detectors are of the transformer type. The Vision Transformer (ViT) treats an image as a sequence of flattened patches, which a conventional Transformer encoder then classifies, and Dosovitskiy et al. demonstrated that such a system could perform as well or better than convolutional networks on large-scale image classification tasks when pre-trained on sufficient data [4]. In October 2022, a proof of concept checkpoint for detecting VQGAN+CLIP style artistic imagery was released called the umm-maybe/AI-image-detector [6]. By contrast, Organika/sdxl-detector adjusts a Swin Transformer to the pairs of images from Wikimedia and SDXL, using the AutoTrain service provided by Hugging Face, and it achieves much better performance on SDXL output at the expense of accuracy on the older images it was trained on [7].

Multiple detector fusion is fairly common to guard against the reality that no single detector covers all the space of generators and simple rules such as majority voting or probability averaging are appealing just because they don't require joint training and remain easy to reason about. With regards to explainability, Simonyan et al. presented gradient-based saliency maps, which are a graphical approximation of the importance of a pixel based on its gradient with respect to the class score of the input image [8]. Later, Selvaraju et al. suggested a technique named Grad-CAM that pools the gradients at a selected convolutional feature layer resulting in a coarser but more class-discriminative heatmap [9]. This paper explicitly mentions that PixelTruth's saliency module is not a feature-layer version of the input-pixel-gradient method from [8] but it is not so in the documentation and interface of PixelTruth itself (which is why it is labeled "Grad-CAM" throughout the project's documentation and interface in this paper).

Additionally, there's a robustness angle to be aware of. The accuracy of a detector deteriorates in the real world, because the generator families are continually new and the detector must be retrained with fresh samples (which is like playing an arms race between the two). Compression, resize, and adversarial light perturbation have been demonstrated in the forensics literature to reduce the accuracy of artifact-based detectors, as artifacts are often high frequency signals that do not withstand compression well. Here, PixelTruth does not try to do anything special; it is just as robust as its two pre-trained checkpoints.

The packaging is as important as a detector's model. Typically, tools designed for non-specialists are delivered in one of three forms, each with its own set of advantages/disadvantages regarding setup, privacy, and costs: a local desktop tool or command-line tool, a hosted web service accessed via a browser, or an API consumed by other software. The second pattern is for a self-hosted, browser-accessed application, like PixelTruth, and is the preferred approach when the image data must be contained within a single deployment boundary, but the app is still accessible via a standard browser tab.

It is good to put this in the context of the larger concern which inspires the field in the first place. Tolosana et al. discuss the related problem of face-manipulation detection, such as face swapping and reenactment using a GAN, and claim that although there are arms races going on between the different manipulation methods and the different detectors, the problem of generalization across manipulation types and unseen datasets is still an open problem and not a solved one [10]. What PixelTruth's two-model ensemble does is in essence a specific example of a pattern detailed in synthetic-media detection literature much more widely.

## III. Proposed Methodology

The PixelTruth approach doesn't actually involve training a new detector -- what's more important -- it just creates a detector by adding together two independently trained checkpoints, using a fusion rule that can be hand-traced, and applying a saliency explanation on top. This section explains the fusion rule used in the model/detector.py module of the project, and then explains the saliency calculation and some of the decisions that were made in implementing both.

The ensemble is a pair of pretrained image-classification pipelines, loaded on the HuggingFace 	ransformers.pipeline interface: $, the umm-maybe/AI-image-detector pipeline based on the ViT model, and $, the Swin-based Organika/sdxl-detector pipeline. Each pipeline takes in an RGB image $ and outputs a set of (label, score) pairs -- the score represents the amount of confidence the model has in that label for the given image. Since the two checkpoints were trained independently, their label strings do not match up so PixelTruth uses simple keyword matching:
- Label string containing 'artificial', 'ai', or 'fake' is classified as **AI-Generated**.
- Label string containing 'human', 'real', or 'photo' is classified as **Real-Image**.

This normalisation step allows two checkpoints, whose label vocabularies are unrelated, to be merged cleanly.

Let ^{\text{AI}}$ and ^{\text{real}}$ be the confidence of the AI-Generated and Real-Image buckets, respectively, for each model $. If the model $ did not vote in that bucket, then ^{\text{AI}}$ and ^{\text{real}}$ are set to 0. The ensemble accumulates these scores for all models:

S_{\text{AI}} = \sum_i s_i^{\text{AI}}, \quad S_{\text{real}} = \sum_i s_i^{\text{real}} \tag{1}

and keeps track of vote counts {\text{AI}}$ and {\text{real}}$, which are incremented each time the score of a model for that bucket passes 0.5. Average confidences follow in a straightforward manner:

\bar{A}_{\text{AI}} = \frac{S_{\text{AI}}}{n}, \quad \bar{A}_{\text{real}} = \frac{S_{\text{real}}}{n} \tag{2}

The system's final decision follows whichever bucket, {\text{AI}}$ or {\text{real}}$, receives the majority of votes, and is the average of the reported confidences of the majority choice. When there are only two models: when they disagree, a tie occurs; when they agree, the tie-break compares the averaged confidences, with the AI-Generated classification taking precedence if they are identical.

`	ext
Algorithm 1: Vote-and-Average Ensemble Fusion
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Input : image x, model set M = {m1, m2}
Output: label in {AI-Generated, Real-Image}, confidence in [0, 100]

S_AI, S_real <- 0, 0
V_AI, V_real <- 0, 0

for each model m_i in M:
    results <- m_i(x)
    for each (label, score) in results:
        bucket <- normalize(label)
        if bucket == AI-Generated:
            S_AI <- S_AI + score
            if score > 0.5: V_AI <- V_AI + 1
        else if bucket == Real-Image:
            S_real <- S_real + score
            if score > 0.5: V_real <- V_real + 1

A_bar_AI   <- S_AI / |M|
A_bar_real <- S_real / |M|

if V_AI > V_real:
    return (AI-Generated, A_bar_AI * 100)
else if V_real > V_AI:
    return (Real-Image, A_bar_real * 100)
else if A_bar_AI >= A_bar_real:
    return (AI-Generated, A_bar_AI * 100)
else:
    return (Real-Image, A_bar_real * 100)
`

### Strategy Comparison

| Strategy | Inference Cost | Coverage / Bias |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| umm-maybe only (ViT) | \times$ forward pass | Biased toward GAN / VQGAN+CLIP era |
| sdxl-detector only (Swin) | \times$ forward pass | Biased toward SDXL-family diffusion |
| **Two-model ensemble (ours)** | **\times$ forward pass** | **Broader coverage; tie-break on disagreement** |

## IV. System Architecture

PixelTruth employs a standard 3-layer architecture consisting of a browser front end, a stateless Flask REST API, and an inference layer that loads and caches both HuggingFace pipelines upon initialization.

`
+-------------------------------------------------------------+
|                        Browser Client                       |
|           (HTML5 / CSS3 / JS Drag-and-Drop Upload UI)       |
+------------------------------+------------------------------+
                               | POST /predict (multipart/form-data)
                               v
+-------------------------------------------------------------+
|                       Flask REST API                        |
|                  (backend/app.py - CORS Enabled)            |
|       - Image validation (Extension, MIME type, Header)     |
|       - In-memory decoding & RGB Normalization (Pillow)     |
+------------------------------+------------------------------+
                               |
        +----------------------+----------------------+
        |                                             |
        v                                             v
+--------------------------------+   +--------------------------------+
|    Two-Model Ensemble Layer    |   |    Gradient-Based Saliency     |
|      (model/detector.py)       |   |      (backend/gradcam.py)      |
|  - Model A: umm-maybe (ViT)    |   |  - Autograd input tracking     |
|  - Model B: Organika (Swin)    |   |  - Top-class backpropagation   |
|  - Vote-and-Average Fusion     |   |  - Heatmap composition         |
+---------------+----------------+   +----------------+---------------+
                |                                     |
                +------------------+------------------+
                                   |
                                   v
+-------------------------------------------------------------+
|                        JSON Response                        |
|  { label, confidence, gradcam_image (b64), original (b64) }|
+-------------------------------------------------------------+
`

The backend (ackend/app.py) is a Flask application enabling CORS with a single primary endpoint: POST /predict. It decodes image bytes in-memory, executes ensemble inference, computes the gradient saliency overlay, and returns base64-encoded PNG representations directly in the JSON response payload. No user images are stored to disk during inference, ensuring complete statelessness and user privacy.

## V. Implementation

The entire system is implemented in Python and vanilla JavaScript with zero build steps or compiler toolchains.

### Technology Stack Summary

| Layer | Technology | Role |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Front end** | HTML5 / CSS3 / Vanilla JS | Drag-and-drop upload UI, verdict rendering, visual inspection |
| **Backend API** | Flask, flask-cors | RESTful endpoint routing, request validation, error formatting |
| **Inference** | HuggingFace 	ransformers, PyTorch | Pretrained ViT & Swin transformer execution |
| **Image I/O** | Pillow (PIL), NumPy | In-memory image decoding, resizing, matrix normalization |
| **Explainability** | PyTorch utograd | Input-gradient saliency map computation & colormap blending |

### Input Validation Matrix

| Condition | HTTP Status | Response Body |
| :--- | :---: | :--- |
| No 'image' field in multipart form | 400 Bad Request | {"error": "No image file provided"} |
| Empty filename | 400 Bad Request | {"error": "Empty filename"} |
| Extension not in allow-list (.png, .jpg, .jpeg, .webp, .gif) | 400 Bad Request | {"error": "Invalid file extension"} |
| Valid request, inference successful | 200 OK | {"label": ..., "confidence": ..., "gradcam_image": ..., "original_image": ...} |
| Unhandled exception during pipeline execution | 500 Server Error | {"error": "<descriptive_message>"} |

In ackend/gradcam.py, the PyTorch model and image processor are extracted from the pipeline. Input gradient tracking is enabled on the pre-processed input tensor, the forward pass is executed, gradients for the target class logit are backpropagated to the input pixels, and the resulting normalized magnitude is converted into an overlaid heatmap using Pillow.

## VI. Experimental Setup

PixelTruth combines two off-the-shelf pretrained checkpoints without retraining or fine-tuning. The experimental evaluation is qualitative and functional: verifying end-to-end reliability across four distinct test categories:

1. **Ordinary camera photographs**: Native high-resolution photographs representing authentic physical scenes.
2. **SDXL-family diffusion images**: Images synthesized by Stable Diffusion XL with fine-grained photorealistic details.
3. **Older GAN / VQGAN+CLIP imagery**: Artifact-heavy synthetic art generated by earlier generative models.
4. **Non-photographic images**: User interface screenshots, vector art, and flat-color digital illustrations.

## VII. Results and Discussion

### Outcome Summary by Image Category

| Image Category | Expected Agreement | Dominant Signal |
| :--- | :---: | :--- |
| **Camera photographs** | High | Both models converge on Real-Image |
| **SDXL-family diffusion** | High | sdxl-detector strongly AI; ensemble follows |
| **Older GAN / VQGAN+CLIP** | Moderate | umm-maybe more decisive; sdxl-detector weaker |
| **Non-photographic (UI / Illustrations)** | Low | Higher disagreement; tie-break rule activated |

### Evaluation Metrics Formulation
For future benchmark testing across labeled test suites, evaluation metrics are formulated as:

\text{Precision} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FP}}, \quad \text{Recall} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FN}} \tag{3}

F_1 = 2 \cdot \frac{\text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}} \tag{4}

\text{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{TP} + \text{TN}}{\text{TP} + \text{FP} + \text{TN} + \text{FN}} \tag{5}

The fusion rule in Algorithm 1 extends overall detection coverage across non-overlapping generator distributions, where the tie-break mechanism operates effectively in boundary regions where individual model domain distributions diverge.

## VIII. Limitations

1. **No Bundled Quantitative Benchmark**: Evaluation is functional and qualitative; formal benchmark precision/recall metrics require dedicated multi-generator test datasets.
2. **Saliency Terminology**: The explainability feature operates as an **input-gradient saliency map** rather than layer-pooled Grad-CAM, highlighting high-frequency edge gradients rather than coarse semantic feature maps.
3. **Single-Model Saliency**: The saliency overlay reflects the gradient of the most recently loaded pipeline rather than a fused attribution across both models.
4. **Stateless Scalability**: The reference Flask deployment runs single-threaded without task queues, rate-limiting, or distributed caching.

---

## IX. Ethical Considerations and Responsible Use

Automated AI detection scores should be interpreted as **indicative rather than authoritative**. PixelTruth is designed to assist human content moderators, journalists, and researchers by providing visual attribution heatmaps alongside probabilistic scores, rather than making unilateral automated determinations. The stateless architecture guarantees that sensitive user uploads are not retained on disk or used for unauthorized data harvesting.

## X. Future Work

1. **True Attention-Rollout / Layer-CAM**: Implement native Vision Transformer attention-rollout and Swin hierarchical feature map activations.
2. **Learned Meta-Classifier**: Train a lightweight stacking logistic regression layer on base model logit outputs rather than heuristic tie-breaking.
3. **Multi-Model Saliency Blending**: Compute joint weighted saliency overlays combining attribution maps from both models.
4. **Temporal Video Extension**: Extend detection pipelines to analyze frame sequences with inter-frame consistency checks.

---

## XI. Conclusion

This paper presented **PixelTruth**, an open-source, dual-transformer ensemble system for detecting AI-generated images. By uniting Vision Transformer (ViT) and Swin Transformer architectures with a deterministic vote-and-average fusion algorithm, gradient-based saliency explainability, and a lightweight Flask REST architecture, PixelTruth demonstrates a practical "composition-first" paradigm for applied machine learning applications.
